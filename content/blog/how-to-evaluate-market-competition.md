Competition is often reduced to a listing count: many results mean “too crowded,” while few results mean “untapped.” Neither conclusion is reliable on its own.

A market with many credible alternatives may have proven buyer intent. A market with no visible alternatives may be underserved, difficult to reach, too small, or not painful enough to support a purchase.

## Define What Customers Actually Compare

Start with the job the customer is trying to complete. Competitors include more than products with the same label.

For a shift-planning template, alternatives might include a spreadsheet, paper calendar, scheduling software, a manager’s existing routine or simply tolerating the problem. These substitutes matter because they already consume the customer’s time, money or attention.

## Build a Small Comparison Set

Select a manageable group of credible alternatives across several types:

- Direct products serving the same customer and job
- Lower-cost or free substitutes
- Premium services or software
- Manual workarounds
- The option of doing nothing

For each alternative, record the audience, promise, price, delivery model, evidence of trust, limitations and likely acquisition channel. Use current product pages or platform documentation rather than copied summaries when possible.

## Separate Observations from Interpretation

An observation is traceable: “this offer includes a printable PDF and an editable spreadsheet.” An interpretation is your conclusion: “buyers may value format flexibility.” The second statement can guide research, but it should not be presented as established fact.

The same rule applies to reviews. A complaint may reveal a useful problem, but one complaint does not establish frequency or willingness to pay.

## Look for Structural Gaps

Weak differentiation usually sounds cosmetic: another color, more pages or a slightly lower price. Stronger possibilities change the customer, job or delivery constraint:

- A general tool adapted to a specific operating environment
- A complicated product reduced to the minimum usable workflow
- A fragmented process combined into one clear handoff
- A product designed around accessibility, regulation or physical conditions
- A service converted into a repeatable self-serve system

Treat each gap as a hypothesis until customers or behavior confirm it.

## Score Competition as Both Evidence and Risk

Competition can support demand while also increasing acquisition cost and reducing differentiation. Record both effects. A useful decision asks:

1. Do credible alternatives show that customers recognize the job?
2. Is there a specific unmet constraint rather than a vague promise to be better?
3. Can the intended customer be reached without competing on price alone?
4. Is the gap large enough to justify switching from the current workaround?

**Practical takeaway:** do not ask whether competition exists. Ask what customers choose today, why they choose it, and what would justify a change.
